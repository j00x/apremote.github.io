Anti-Aging — Urolithin A & Senolytics

Two mechanisms dominate the human data right now: mitophagy (urolithin A) and senescent-cell clearance (senolytics). Both show biological signals, but neither has proven it slows human aging.

  • Urolithin A hits the aging immune system. A double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT in Nature Aging gave 50 healthy middle-aged adults 1,000 mg/day of urolithin A for 4 weeks. It expanded naive-like, less-exhausted CD8+ T cells, boosted their fatty-acid-oxidation capacity and mitochondrial biogenesis, and improved monocyte function — early evidence it may counter "inflammaging."
  • But keep expectations modest. A systematic review of 5 human trials (250 people, 10–1,000 mg/day, up to 4 months) found a dose-dependent anti-inflammatory effect and gains in muscle strength and endurance, but no change in physical function, cardiovascular outcomes, or gut microbiota. Adverse events were mild.
  • Senolytics stay experimental. A phase-2 RCT of intermittent dasatinib + quercetin in 60 postmenopausal women missed its primary endpoint (no change in the bone-resorption marker CTx at 20 weeks). It was safe, but the "clear senescent cells to rejuvenate" story isn't yet backed by human outcomes.

Practical framing: urolithin A is the best-supported single supplement here for muscle and inflammatory markers — expect modest effects. Treat rapamycin, fisetin, and D+Q as research-stage, not routine.

No. 02 · Fitness

HIIT Workouts for Women

New 2026 evidence keeps HIIT as a time-efficient option for women — especially after menopause — while nudging toward adding resistance work for the biggest body-composition wins.

  • Postmenopausal RCT (2026). 45 women aged 50–65 with overweight/obesity were randomized to HIIT, concurrent training (cardio + strength), or control for 12 weeks, 3×/week. Both exercise groups significantly cut body weight, BMI, abdominal fat, triglycerides, LDL, glucose and insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) vs. control. Concurrent training produced the greater drop in weight, BMI and body-fat %, while metabolic gains were similar — a case for pairing intervals with lifting.
  • HIIT is genuinely time-efficient. An earlier RCT showed 28 minutes of HIIT, 3×/week, reduced visceral fat more than 60 minutes of combined walking + resistance in obese postmenopausal women.

Bottom line: intensity drives the cardio-metabolic benefit, but don't skip strength training — the combined approach edged out HIIT alone on fat loss. Progress volume gradually and keep recovery adequate.

No. 03 · Women's Health

Pelvic Floor Exercises

Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) remains the evidence-based first-line treatment for stress urinary incontinence (SUI). This week's studies clarify when biofeedback actually helps.

  • New 2026 sham-controlled RCT. 60 women were randomized to PFMT alone, EMG-biofeedback + PFMT, or sham-biofeedback + PFMT (3×/week, 8 weeks). Both the real and sham biofeedback groups improved incontinence frequency, pad-test leakage, quality of life and muscle activation more than PFMT alone — suggesting the supervision and structured repetition matter as much as the device itself.
  • Meta-analysis (2026). Pooling 8 RCTs (n≈1,045), adding EMG biofeedback to PFMT gave a small reduction in incontinence severity and QoL, with a moderate gain in pelvic-floor strength — supporting selective use, especially for women who can't identify the correct contraction.
  • The big long-term trial disagrees on routine use. The 600-woman OPAL trial found no meaningful difference at 24 months between PFMT + biofeedback and PFMT alone — so routine biofeedback isn't recommended for everyone.
  • Home devices can help specific groups. In 452 women with postpartum SUI, adding a home pressure-mediated biofeedback device to supervised PFMT more than doubled the cure rate vs. PFMT alone.

Key point: correct technique and consistency beat gadgets. Confirm activation with a clinician; reserve biofeedback for those who struggle to isolate the muscles or want home reinforcement.

No. 04 · Oncology

Integrative Angles — HER2+ / TP53 Breast Cancer

"Alternative" is best read as evidence-based integrative care alongside standard HER2-targeted therapy — never a replacement. Two 2026 threads are worth noting.

  • p53 genetics matter in this group. A 2026 genomic analysis of 224 breast DCIS cases found TP53 mutations were particularly prevalent, reinforcing that p53 status tracks with more aggressive, often HER2-driven biology — a reason to take germline/somatic testing seriously when planning treatment and surveillance.
  • The targeted-therapy backbone keeps advancing. New work on HER2 antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) resistance (e.g., trastuzumab-rezetecan / SHR-A1811) is testing nanotheranostics that trigger immunogenic pyroptosis and reprogram tumor-associated macrophages to overcome resistance — context for why integrative options are add-ons, not substitutes, for HER2-directed drugs.
  • Natural compounds: mechanism, not proof. A 2026 xenograft study of a carvacrol-enriched multicomponent oil reduced tumor growth in a triple-negative model — the kind of preclinical signal that fuels supplement interest but is far from clinical evidence. Bioavailability and drug–supplement interactions with targeted therapy are real concerns.

Important framing: prioritize standard HER2-targeted treatment; discuss any supplement (vitamin D repletion, omega-3s, etc.) with your oncology team before starting, since some interact with therapy. Much integrative data is early, observational, or preclinical.

No. 05 · Sleep

Sleep — Studies & Best Practices

For chronic, restless, or fragmented sleep, 2026 guidance keeps behavioral therapy ahead of pills and supplements.

  • New multi-society position statement (2026). A joint statement from six Australian sleep, GP, psychology, pharmacy and nursing bodies reaffirms CBT-I (cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia) as first-line for adult insomnia, with medications reserved as short-term adjuncts and healthy-sleep practices for everyone.
  • Supplements, honestly. Melatonin is best for circadian problems (jet lag, shift work, older adults with delayed sleep onset) rather than chronic insomnia; a 2026 pharmacology review even explores dietary melatonin sources for broader health. Evidence for magnesium in insomnia remains limited and low-quality — don't expect much.

Best-practice habits for restless/disjointed sleep:

  1. Keep a fixed wake time, even on weekends — it anchors your circadian rhythm.
  2. Reserve the bed for sleep only; keep the room cool, dark, and quiet.
  3. Dim lights and cut screens during a wind-down window; get morning daylight.
  4. If disrupted sleep persists beyond a few weeks, see a provider to rule out sleep apnea and consider CBT-I (in-person or digital) before hypnotics.
