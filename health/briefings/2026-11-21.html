Anti-Aging — Supplements, Topicals & Devices

The theme this cycle: match the hype to the evidence tier. No supplement has yet proven it extends human lifespan, but the human data are maturing unevenly.

  • Longevity supplements, ranked by evidence. A 2026 comparative review (Journal of Biosciences and Medicines) weighed urolithin A, spermidine, and NAD+ precursors (NR/NMN). Urolithin A had the most coherent profile — aligned mechanism, biomarker, and early functional data for mitochondrial health, muscle endurance, and immune aging. NR/NMN reliably raise NAD+ biomarkers but show inconsistent clinical benefit. Spermidine remains biologically plausible but clinically unvalidated.
  • Urolithin A, newest RCT. A 2025 Nature Aging double-blind RCT (50 healthy middle-aged adults, 1,000 mg/day for 4 weeks) found UA shifted CD8+ T cells toward a less-exhausted, naive-like state and boosted mitochondrial biogenesis — a plausible counter to "inflammaging," though endpoints were immunologic, not clinical.
  • Rapamycin reality check. The 48-week PEARL RCT of intermittent low-dose rapamycin (5–10 mg weekly) was safe, but the primary endpoint — visceral fat — did not change. Only a signal in women on 10 mg (lean mass, less pain). Don't expect off-label rapamycin to "reverse aging."
  • Senolytics stay investigational. A 2026 systematic review of senolytics/senomorphics in humans found only ~6 completed studies; dasatinib + quercetin showed preliminary functional and biomarker effects, but evidence is insufficient to call them established therapies.
  • Topicals & devices. For photoaging, topical tretinoin still has the strongest wrinkle evidence (2024 meta-analysis); OTC retinol evidence is weak. A gentler alternative: a 2026 study of a home-use red + near-infrared LED mask (630/850 nm) plus cream reported −24% crow's-feet depth and improved radiance at 8 weeks, with higher collagen I/elastin ex vivo — promising, but the clinical arm lacked a control group.

Practical framing: prefer interventions with human RCTs, expect modest effects, and treat single-supplement "longevity" claims skeptically.

HIIT Workouts for Women

Fresh 2026 syntheses keep HIIT as a time-efficient cardio and fat-loss tool for women — with a consistent caveat to pair it with resistance training and read the effect sizes modestly.

  • Women with overweight/obesity. A 2026 systematic review & meta-analysis in Frontiers in Physiology (15 RCTs) found HIIT vs. no structured exercise significantly reduced body-fat % (g = −0.76), fat mass (g = −0.66), BMI and body weight, and strongly improved VO₂max (g = 1.09). It also nudged HDL up and LDL down; effects on total cholesterol/triglycerides were not significant. Note: evidence certainty was rated low to very low.
  • Recreationally active women. A separate meta-analysis (15 studies, ~304 women) found HIIT improved VO₂max/VO₂peak and lowered body-fat %, but did not change total body mass or fat mass — expect recomposition more than scale movement.
  • Which exercise for fat? A 2026 BMJ Open network meta-analysis (43 trials) ranked HIIT favorably for reducing body-fat % and fat mass, while finding that no aerobic intervention meaningfully increased lean mass — the practical argument for adding strength work alongside HIIT.

Bottom line: intensity drives the cardio and adiposity wins; add resistance training for lean mass, progress volume gradually, and keep recovery adequate.

Pelvic Floor Exercises

Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) remains the evidence-based first-line treatment for stress urinary incontinence (SUI). The newest trials reinforce specificity and explore add-ons.

  • PFMT beats Pilates, head-to-head. A 2026 RCT in the International Urogynecology Journal (105 women, 12 supervised sessions over 6 weeks) found PFMT clearly superior to Pilates — with or without pelvic-floor contraction cueing. Incontinence impact (ICIQ-SF) dropped −4.4 points with PFMT vs. just −0.7 for Pilates, and peak vaginal squeeze pressure rose +24.4 mmHg vs. ~6. Targeted training outperforms general core work.
  • Electrical stimulation as an add-on. A 2026 Scientific Reports RCT (30 women) added interferential current (IFC) to PFM exercises. It produced a significant extra reduction in weekly leakage frequency, but no added benefit for pressure, severity, or quality of life — a narrow bonus, not a replacement for the exercises.
  • Postpartum: combine the drills. A 2026 trial in post-vaginal-delivery SUI found combining focused (Kegel) + generalized PFMT produced the greatest strength gains and a lower 6-month recurrence rate than either alone.

Key point: correct activation and consistency beat sheer reps. Confirm technique with a clinician or biofeedback; supervised programs generally outperform unsupervised ones.

Alternative & Integrative Options — HER2+ / TP53 Breast Cancer

"Alternative" is safest read as evidence-based integrative care alongside standard treatment — not a replacement for it. Two threads stand out for HER2-positive (often TP53/p53-altered) disease.

  • A genotype-matched strategy (early-stage science). A 2026 npj Breast Cancer study showed HER2 + TP53 co-mutated tumors carry increased histone acetylation and chromatin accessibility, making them sensitive to the TOP1-inhibitor payload in trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd). In organoid/mouse models, neratinib + T-DXd was synergistic. This is preclinical; the combination is now in a phase I trial (NCT05372614) — encouraging context, not current standard care.
  • Integrative care that is actually supported. Society for Integrative Oncology guidelines endorse mind-body therapies — meditation, yoga, music therapy, stress management — for anxiety, mood, and quality of life, and acupuncture/acupressure for chemo-induced nausea. There is no strong evidence that ingested dietary supplements treat the cancer itself.
  • Safety first with supplements. NIH's NCCIH and the 2026 AGO guideline stress that no complementary approach cures cancer, some herbs can blunt chemotherapy, and anything ingested should be cleared with the oncology team to avoid drug interactions. Because germline TP53 (Li-Fraumeni) carriers face added radiation sensitivity and second-cancer risk, genetics should guide decisions here.

Important framing: use integrative options as add-ons discussed with an oncologist — never as substitutes for HER2-targeted therapy.

Sleep — Studies & Best Practices

For restless or fragmented sleep, 2026 guidance still puts behavioral therapy ahead of pills and supplements.

  • CBT-I stays first-line. A 2026 multi-society Australian position statement reaffirms cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the first-line treatment, effective across settings and delivery formats (in-person, group, tele-health, and self-guided digital).
  • What inside CBT-I actually works. A large component network meta-analysis (241 trials, ~31,000 adults, ~67% women) found the key active ingredients are cognitive restructuring, third-wave skills, sleep restriction, and stimulus control. Notably for disjointed sleep, sleep restriction and stimulus control improved wake-after-sleep-onset and sleep efficiency. Sleep-hygiene advice alone was not sufficient, and standalone relaxation may be counterproductive. In-person, therapist-led delivery was most effective.
  • Supplements, honestly. Per NIH/NCCIH, melatonin can shorten time to fall asleep and ease daytime sleepiness but does not improve overnight awakenings or sleep quality — most useful for circadian issues (jet lag, shift work). A 2026 systematic review concluded current evidence does not support magnesium as a routine insomnia treatment (low/very-low certainty).

Best-practice habits for restless/disjointed sleep:

  1. Keep a fixed wake time, even on weekends.
  2. Use the bed for sleep only; keep the room cool, dark, and quiet (stimulus control).
  3. If you're lying awake, get up and return when sleepy rather than clock-watching.
  4. If disrupted sleep persists beyond a few weeks, see a provider to rule out sleep apnea and consider CBT-I (in-person or a validated digital program).
